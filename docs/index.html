    Behind the
    data.

    Two public sources, one question. The numbers on this page come from the outputs of the project’s own scripts. Valence is Spotify’s 0–1 score for how positive a track sounds — the target here. Popularity is not part of this study.

    1. 01

      Main dataset

      194 songs × 29 columns from the studio albums. The lyrics column arrived empty and there is no popularity column.

    2. 02

      Cleaning

      3 bonus tracks without audio features removed and the empty column dropped, leaving 191 songs × 28 columns.

    3. 03

      Lyrics join

      A second source, matched by title, gives 130 songs across 8 albums. It stops at folklore.

    4. 04

      Features

      9 audio features, plus text features (sentiment, vocabulary diversity, TF-IDF). Only derived numbers are stored.

    5. 05

      Modeling

      Ridge and Random Forest, validated with shuffled folds and with whole albums held out.

    This story uses two sample sizes, 191 songs for the audio-only experiments and 130 songs for anything involving lyrics. They are never mixed in a comparison.

    Chapter 04

    Can we predict
    how a song feels?

    This is a regression problem. We use what a song sounds like to estimate its valence, then check the estimate on songs the model has never seen.

    Predictors9 audio features (danceability, energy, loudness, speechiness, acousticness, liveness, tempo, duration and mode)

    Validation5 shuffled folds, and the stricter test of holding whole albums out

    A song becomes nine numbers describing how it sounds……each weighted by the model, with energy and acousticness carrying the most weight……and the result is read out as a predicted valence, to be compared with the real one.

    Energy, loudness and acousticness are strongly correlated with each other, so read the weights as a group, not one by one.

    How good is the prediction?

    R² is the share of the variation in valence that the model explains. It is not a percentage of correct predictions. A value of 0 means no better than guessing the average and 1 would be perfect.

    R² — higher is better

    Mean absolute error, in valence points — lower is better

    Holding out whole albums is the harder test because the model never hears a single song from the album it is predicting. R² drops from 0.40 to 0.33 — still above the baseline, but the shuffled-fold number was optimistic. Random Forest was only run with shuffled folds, so compare it with Ridge’s 0.40, not 0.33.

    Chapter 05

    Do lyrics
    add something?

    The lyrics source covers 130 songs from 8 albums (it stops at folklore, so evermore and Midnights are missing). All comparisons below use these same 130 songs, with whole albums held out. The lyrics themselves are never shown or stored in the repository — only numbers derived from them.

    VADER sentiment

    A rule-based score of how positive or negative the wording is. Built for short social-media text, so a rough fit for song lyrics.

    Vocabulary diversity

    Unique words divided by total words, plus word count and share of repeated lines.

    TF-IDF

    Up to 300 words that characterise each song against the others (kept only if used in 3+ songs), added to Ridge at three penalty strengths.

    Audio alone vs audio + TF-IDF

    audio onlyaudio + TF-IDFTF-IDF only

    Simple text features

    audio onlyaudio + simple texttext only

    No consistent gain from lyrics. Audio + TF-IDF roughly ties audio alone (0.23 vs 0.24, 0.22 vs 0.22) and is lower at the weakest penalty (0.15 vs 0.21), while simple text features lower it (0.10 vs 0.21). Text on its own does no better than the mean baseline. With 8 albums the fold-to-fold spread is huge (up to ±0.46 for the simple features), so small gaps here are noise — the safe reading is that there is no evidence lyrics help.

    How each text feature relates to valence

    Correlation on the 130 songs — exploratory only.

    Negative-word share correlates positively with valence (0.24) and the overall sentiment score negatively (−0.21). That is the opposite of what the words alone would suggest, which is consistent with sad lyrics over upbeat sound — but it is a hypothesis, not a result of this study.

    The model over-estimates the mood of the two darkest albums. It predicts about 0.42 for reputation (actual 0.29) and 0.39 for Midnights (actual 0.28). Predictions are also compressed toward the middle, so the model has trouble at the extremes.

    Music is more than numbers.

    What we found

    • Audio explains part of the variation in valence. Ridge reaches R² 0.33 with whole albums held out (191 songs), while the mean baseline sits near zero.
    • It beats the baseline on 9 of 10 albums (error 0.125 vs 0.157), with folklore as a tie.
    • Lyrics added no consistent gain on the 130 songs that have them.
    • Generalisation varies by era. reputation and Midnights are over-estimated by about 0.13 and 0.10.
    • The sample is small, so these are exploratory findings, not universal ones. Correlation is not causation, and prediction is not explanation.

    Limitations

    • 191 songs, with folds of only 1–2 albums (130 songs and 8 albums with lyrics), so the spread across folds is large.
    • Lyrics stop at folklore, so evermore and Midnights are not in the text experiments.
    • Audio for Fearless, Red and Speak Now comes from the Taylor’s Version re-recordings.
    • Valence is itself a score computed by Spotify, not a human rating.
    • The Ridge penalty was compared on the same data used for evaluation, which can flatter the best setting.
    • VADER was designed for short social-media text.

    Sources & method

    • Audio features and album metadata come from TidyTuesday 2023-10-17, taylor_album_songs (from the R taylor package, with Spotify API data).
    • Lyrics come from TidyTuesday 2020-09-29, taylor_swift_lyrics. They are not redistributed and only derived numbers are versioned.
    • The scripts are limpeza.py, eda.py, modelo_valencia.py, avaliacao.py, features_texto.py, modelo_texto.py, modelo_tfidf.py, por_album.py, dashboard.py.
